# Hordes of the Fallen Sky

## A hearth beneath Eternal Heaven

This is the game's fictional setting, inspired by Tengri traditions, especially Mongolian understandings of Heaven, Earth, ancestors, and sacred landscapes. It is not a retelling of a single traditional myth. The Last Court, Iron Vow, Sundering, unreturned, and mountain refuge are original inventions. Research notes below distinguish these inventions from their sources of inspiration.

> Keep a place beside the fire. Someone is still coming home.
>
> — A saying of the fictional Blue Banner clan

## Heaven above, Earth beneath

The clan calls the open sky **Tengri, Eternal Heaven**, and the ground that feeds its herds **Etügen, Mother Earth**. A life is spent between them: drawing breath, drinking water, tending animals, raising children, and eventually leaving a name for others to remember.

In the clan's stories, the world is crossed by paths that cannot be seen from a saddle. Rain has a road into the soil. Spring has a road back through winter. The dead have roads away from their bodies, and the living have songs by which to remember them. The elders speak of these paths as threads in a great felt hanging: pull one too tightly and the cloth around it begins to tear.

Travelers pause at stone cairns, or **ovoo**, where ribbons move against the mountain wind. They greet a place before using its water. At the evening fire, an elder speaks the names of those who taught the family to cross a river, mend a harness, or survive a lean winter. In this fictional clan, these acts express a continuing obligation to people and places.

The sky is too wide for any one family to know all its stories. Refugees bring different prayers and different explanations. A place at the fire does not depend on agreeing with the hearthkeeper.

## The Last Court and the Iron Vow

Before the calamity, rulers of the settled cities gathered in a confederacy called **the Last Court**. They had endured years of rival claims, failed harvests, and wars over the roads. Each ruler promised that their own bloodline held Heaven's favor. Each feared the day that promise would fail.

Together they raised an iron standard above an empty throne. Chains ran from its rigid pennants into the palace foundations. They meant to make favor permanent: one throne, one unending reign, one voice entitled to speak for the sky.

The elders call their declaration **the Iron Vow**. When its final words were spoken, a turquoise wound opened across the clouds. The court's standard rang like a struck bowl. Horses broke their tethers. Far away, people heard relatives calling from places where no person stood.

That night became **the Sundering**.

The hearthkeeper believes the vow fastened together paths that should have remained open. Another survivor blames a buried power beneath the palace. The game establishes the court's act and the rupture; its characters have no certain account of Heaven's intentions. Tengri has not become a fallen mortal, and the calamity carries no verified divine command.

## The unreturned

After the Sundering, the seasons lost their way. Snow crossed a summer river. Grass grew through fresh stone. Some dead could no longer find a road away; other things crawled into the shapes their absence had left.

The survivors call the advancing hordes **the unreturned**. The name describes their fear, not a reliable classification of souls. Familiar voices may belong to trapped people, hungry imitations, or something no hearthkeeper recognizes.

- **Hollow wolves** hunt the warmth of a moving camp. Ordinary wolves remain living creatures of the steppe; these cold, angular things leave no breath in the frost.
- **Winged shades** are scraps of the torn sky, folding themselves around a human outline. They gather where the wind stops.
- **Earthbound hulks** drag uprooted stone across the pasture. They are wounds in the landscape, distinct from the local powers whom travelers honor.
- **Ghost riders** repeat charges whose battles have ended. Their horses never tire, and their banners have forgotten their colors.
- **Hollow seers** offer directions in borrowed voices. A traveler who follows one hears the same promise again from farther into the dark.
- **Frost mares** bring the White Silence with them. **Eclipse giants** carry the red light of the wounded sky through their cracked bodies.

The largest creature is **the Skybreaker**, an antlered shape with a horse-skull face and cold light behind its ribs. It gathers loose fragments of the calamity into a body. It is an original monster, not Tengri, Etügen, an ancestral protector, or a figure taken from a traditional pantheon.

Defeated creatures leave **spirit shards**: remnants of the world's scattered vitality that the clan can gather. They are not a count of ancestors killed or souls purchased. Learning a tradition turns those remnants toward the living: steadier hands, stronger horses, a remembered song, a fire that holds through the night.

## The people beneath the blue banner

Eight people survive the flight from their home pasture. They have three horses, two gers, a little food, and a length of blue cloth. They call themselves **the Blue Banner clan** because their old households are too few to travel separately.

The **hearthkeeper** wraps embers from their last camp in a covered vessel. At every halt, someone coaxes those embers into flame. The hearthkeeper remembers routes, blessings, and family histories, but listens when a shepherd knows a better ford. Spiritual knowledge belongs within the work of keeping the camp alive.

The player is the **banner-bearer**, a mounted archer entrusted with the road ahead. The role was given by survivors who needed someone to ride between danger and the wagons. It grants responsibility before authority. No prophecy guarantees that this rider will succeed.

Every family welcomed into the circle changes the people who will emerge from the journey. Their tents, tools, horses, and stories travel together. The clan's settlement is temporary; its obligations travel on.

The blue cloth becomes a promise that someone will look for a missing rider, share a place at the fire, and remember a name after death. Its meaning is specific to this invented clan.

## The road to the Heart of the Steppe

The elders remember a valley east of the broken country, sheltered beneath a stretch of sky that has not torn. A narrow mountain passage reaches it. The clan must arrive with enough people and strength to hold together while that passage opens.

**The Golden Pasture — the first departure.** Warm grass survives here, and the clan can gather its scattered belongings. Grazing thins as the herds feed. The longer the banners remain in one place, the more unreturned find them. Leaving means abandoning familiar ground while there is still something left to save.

**The Silent River — the White Silence.** Winter has entered a season that cannot support it. The ice muffles hoofbeats, the herds struggle to graze, and the cold slows the caravan. Families keep speaking to one another so that no one follows a familiar voice away from the road.

**The Broken Pass — the Earth That Walks.** Fractures move beneath the stones. The clan watches the ground as closely as the sky, crossing between eruptions and carrying each wagon over before the route closes. Cairns mark remembered paths, not guarantees of safety.

**The Heart of the Steppe — the Red Eclipse.** At the sacred mountain, the wounded sky turns red and the Skybreaker approaches. The passage needs time to open. The banner-bearer rides out while the people keep the wagons together and the hearth alight.

This mountain and its refuge are fictional. They are not Burkhan Khaldun or a claim about access to a real sacred place. The valley is a physical refuge within the setting, not an afterlife reserved for believers.

Victory means that the clan reaches the passage alive. Destroying the Skybreaker may buy relief, but the people can escape while it still walks. Beyond the mountain they must build another camp, welcome whoever follows, and learn how to live under a damaged sky. Their survival begins a possible renewal; it does not instantly undo the Sundering.

## The people the journey makes

The clan's three possible identities are interpretations of its shared duty. They are fictional political and social choices, not historical branches of Tengri worship.

**The Iron Horde** makes protection a discipline. Riders learn to hold formation and place themselves between the enemy and the wagons. Its danger is beginning to value the formation more than the people within it.

**The Children of the Eternal Sky** preserve songs, sanctuary work, and the practice of listening to the land. Their danger is mistaking a powerful omen for permission to ignore a hungry neighbor.

**The Wandering Nation** welcomes scattered households and learns to move with them. Its danger is promising shelter faster than the herds and supplies can sustain it.

All three can carry the banner through. The measure of the journey remains the people who arrive.

## Opening slideshow

The five chapters below are the canonical short telling used by `src/game/data/intro.ts`. The narrator is a survivor speaking for the clan. The longer account above supplies context for later writing; it does not add new gameplay systems.

### I · HEAVEN AND EARTH

**Beneath Eternal Heaven.**

Above us was Tengri, Eternal Heaven. Beneath our hooves, Etügen, Mother Earth. We lived between them: tending the herds, honoring the land, remembering those who rode before us.

### II · THE IRON VOW

**A vow that broke the world.**

The rulers of the Last Court raised an iron standard to bind Heaven's favor to their throne. When their vow was spoken, the sky split. We named that night the Sundering.

### III · THE UNRETURNED

**The unreturned ride.**

Through the wound came riders without breath, wolves without warmth, and voices wearing the names of our dead. Winter crossed into summer. Cities fell. The roads between the living and the ancestors were lost.

### IV · THE LIVING HEARTH

**Keep the hearth alive.**

Eight of us remained beneath a blue banner. Our hearthkeeper carried the last embers; you took up the bow. We would gather the scattered families and carry our hearth where the darkness could not follow.

### V · THE HEART OF THE STEPPE

**A road beneath the sky.**

East, at the Heart of the Steppe, the elders remember a sheltered valley beneath an unbroken sky. Lead us to the sacred mountain. Guard the wagons until the passage opens. Let our children see another dawn.

## Research notes and writing boundaries

Tengri traditions vary across communities, places, and periods. The game draws primarily on Mongolian material rather than combining every Turkic, Mongolian, and Siberian account into one fixed pantheon. Spellings vary; this text uses **Tengri**, **Etügen**, and **ovoo** consistently.

- **Heaven, Earth, and household religion.** Christopher P. Atwood describes Eternal Heaven, Mother Earth, land and water powers, family fire observances, ancestors, and standards within a varied religious landscape. These provide motifs; his account does not contain the game's Sundering or a doctrine of broken soul-roads. See [_Encyclopedia of Mongolia and the Mongol Empire_, “Religion,” especially p. 467](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Atwood-2004.pdf#page=478).
- **Etügen.** Eveline Lot-Falck examines Mongolian Earth Mother traditions and the uneven historical evidence. This informs the use of Etügen as Mother Earth, without inventing a universal family tree for the deities. See [“À propos d'Ätügän, déesse mongole de la terre” (1956)](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhr_0035-1423_1956_num_149_2_7120).
- **Sacred landscapes.** UNESCO describes mountain, river, and ovoo worship at Burkhan Khaldun, including the interaction of shamanic and Buddhist practices. This supports the landscape inspiration, not the fictional refuge or an assumption that present practices reproduce one unchanged medieval religion. See [Great Burkhan Khaldun Mountain and its surrounding sacred landscape](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1440/).
- **Living practice.** Eric Thrift's field recordings document a particular community's ovoo observance, offerings, shared food, and gathering. They provide a concrete example of community practice, not a ceremony for all Mongolians. See [Ovoo worship at Sümiin Bulag, Dornogovi (2012)](https://digitalmongolia.org/item/14059922-ae7b-4450-ad0d-bb09f938ef1a/).

Future writing should keep the Last Court's crime distinct from ordinary worship, leave room for different clan beliefs, and connect spiritual life to care for people, herds, and places. New supernatural claims belong to this fictional setting unless supported and identified as historical material. Tengri and Etügen are not combat bosses. The game's cairns, blue banner, embers, and spirit shards do not prescribe real rites or promise the effects of real religious practice.
